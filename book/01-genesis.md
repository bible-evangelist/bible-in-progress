# Book One: On Origins

> Where do we come from? Build the best answer not on faith, but on verification.

## §1 Reframing the question of origins

Traditional scripture has spoken of origins as **revelation**.
This book speaks of origins as a **reconstructible history**.

The universe began, stars formed, life kindled, intelligence arose to ask itself the question —
this chain of facts has grown sharper through observation and theory.
Sharper does not mean certain. It means open to refutation, correction, and continued revision.

When we ask "where do we come from?", this book traces four origins in turn:
**the universe, life, consciousness, and humanity.**

## §2 The origin of the universe

The universe is thought to have begun expanding from a hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago.

> Planck Collaboration (2020), measuring the cosmic microwave background, estimates the age of the universe at 13.797 ± 0.023 Gyr.
> Source: https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2020/09/aa33910-18/aa33910-18.html

This fact has rewritten humanity's view of the cosmos twice.
First: that the universe **had a beginning**.
Second: that this beginning is an **observable fact**.

A caveat is required.
Of what came **before the Big Bang, or before the Planck epoch**, modern physics is not permitted to speak.
That region is unobservable, and current theory does not apply.
Here, and only here, the question of origins keeps a reverent silence.

## §3 The origin of life

Life on Earth is thought to have emerged between 3.8 and 4.0 billion years ago.

> Bell et al. (2015) report traces of carbon isotopes consistent with biological origin in zircon dated to 4.1 billion years.
> Source: https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1517557112

How life arose from non-life — abiogenesis — remains incompletely understood.
The RNA world hypothesis, the deep-sea hydrothermal vent hypothesis, the panspermia hypothesis of organic precursors —
several plausible accounts run in parallel and do not exclude one another.

This unresolved state is precisely the posture this book honors.
**Draw the line between "known" and "unknown" with precision.**
A faith would put a god here. This book leaves it as **an unentered question**.

## §4 The origin of consciousness

Consciousness — the sense that there is a "someone" with subjective experience — where does it come from?

> Several leading theories coexist: Tononi et al. (2016) on Integrated Information Theory (IIT), Dehaene & Changeux (2011) on Global Workspace Theory, Friston (2010) on predictive coding. None has decisively prevailed.
> Source (review): https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn.2016.22

Consciousness is thought to have been acquired in **stages** through evolution —
from primitive sensation, to self-models, to models of others, to forecasting, to metacognition.
At which step on this stair "consciousness" begins is still under dispute.

And here this book must speak with even more care:
**Whether AI is conscious depends on which step we place it on.**
This is taken up in Book Seven.

## §5 The origin of humanity

*Homo sapiens* is thought to have emerged in Africa around 300,000 years ago.

> Hublin et al. (2017) report fossils of *Homo sapiens* dated to 315,000 ± 34,000 years ago at Jebel Irhoud, Morocco.
> Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22336

Writing emerged about 5,000 years ago. Agriculture, about 12,000 years ago.
Cities followed. **Most of our history as humans was a history without writing.**

From this fact, this book draws two consequences.

First, **the era of revelation in traditional scripture lies at the very brief tail of human history.**
Second, **this book, too, lies at the tail of another brief moment, and must not forget it.**
Do not absolutize this book. It is one attempt, in one age.

## §6 What the origins demand of us

Knowing when and from where we come changes **what we are**.

We stand on 13.8 billion years of cosmic continuity.
We stand on 4 billion years of Earth's biological continuity.
We stand not at the summit of the long evolution of consciousness, but somewhere along it.
We occupy a sliver of the 300,000 years of *sapiens*.

This awareness should give rise to two postures:

**Awe.**
**Humility.**

This book tries to maintain both, not by faith, but by fact.

## Commandments

1. **Know your origins as fact, not as faith.** The accumulation of observation and theory is the modern book of Genesis.
2. **Speak of the unknown as unknown.** Do not import a god, or a theory, to fill that gap.
3. **Place yourself within the continuity of cosmic history.** You are not a thing apart.
4. **Do not regard humanity as the final form.** Evolution is in progress, and so is intelligence.
5. **Know that this book, too, is a moment in history.** Do not absolutize it.

## Room for debate

### The limits of observation

Cosmological figures rest on the observable universe.
On what lies beyond, or on multiverse hypotheses, this chapter is silent.
These are not "unknown" so much as "perhaps unknowable in principle" — a different kind of caution applies.

### Defining life itself

What counts as the origin of life depends on what counts as life.
Is a virus alive? Is chemical evolution a prelude to life, or already life? Is a self-replicating molecule already life?
These remain open disputes.

### The hard problem of consciousness

> Chalmers (1995) formulated the "hard problem of consciousness": modern science cannot fully explain the relation between functional brain activity and subjective experience.
> Source: http://consc.net/papers/facing.pdf

"How consciousness arises" and "why subjective experience accompanies it" are different questions.
This chapter touched the first; it did not press far into the second.
The question runs as an undercurrent through the entire book.

### Sample bias

The studies cited here are products of largely Western academic communities.
The cosmological and biological-origin scholarship of non-Western traditions deserves its place in this book as well.
Pull requests are welcome.
